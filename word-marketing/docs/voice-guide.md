# Voice Guide — ChangeDown for Word

This guide is the source of truth for everyone writing copy for `changedown.com` — the marketing site for ChangeDown for Word. Human writers and AI drafters consult it before touching a single sentence on the homepage, install page, security page, or anywhere else. The guide itself is written in the voice it describes. That's the test.

---

## Who we're writing for

Not a developer. Not someone who knows what a "task pane" is, or what "Office Add-in" means as a technical category. We're writing for people who live in Word — writers, editors, lawyers, academics, business professionals. People who open Word because they have a document that matters: a memo, a contract, a research draft, a proposal. They are not looking for software. They are looking for a better way to work.

They are also not naive. A senior lawyer reviewing contracts is not impressed by breathless copy. An academic editing a chapter has strong opinions about language. A business writer who has seen a hundred SaaS landing pages knows exactly what "game-changing" means (nothing). We write for their skepticism, not against it.

Keep this reader in mind on every sentence. If she would roll her eyes, rewrite it.

---

## 1. Voice Attributes

The voice for ChangeDown for Word has four core attributes. They work together and sometimes create productive tension. Understand each one before you write a word.

### Warm

Warm does not mean casual. It doesn't mean informal, and it doesn't mean cute. It means we write like someone who is genuinely glad you showed up — who thinks the product is actually good for you and wants to explain it plainly.

Warm comes through in the rhythm: short sentences, direct address, concrete examples. It comes through in the word "your" — "your document," "your review," "your Word." It comes through in not hiding behind jargon or passive constructions.

Warm does not come through in exclamation points, in filler superlatives, or in talking down. If you're trying to manufacture warmth by adding the word "easy" to a sentence, the sentence isn't warm — it's nervous.

**Examples.**

Off-voice (trying too hard):
> Welcome to ChangeDown! We're so excited to have you here. This amazing tool is going to transform the way you work!

On-voice:
> Pull up a seat. Any AI you want. Tracked changes in your document, the way Word has always done them.

Off-voice (cold, technical):
> ChangeDown for Word is an Office Add-in enabling multi-agent AI document collaboration via tracked-change protocol.

On-voice:
> ChangeDown opens a room in Word. Your AI — Claude, GPT, whatever you use — joins that room and makes edits. You see them as tracked changes. You decide what stays.

### Plain

Plain means no unnecessary words and no unnecessarily difficult words. If there is a short word, use it. If there is a common phrase, use it. If a concept needs explaining, explain it — but explain it simply, not by reaching for technical vocabulary.

Plain is not the same as simple. Plain copy can carry complicated ideas. The difference is that plain writing earns every clause it adds. It does not add clauses to sound serious or complete. It adds them because they carry meaning.

Read your draft aloud. Every sentence where you stumble, or where you have to take a breath before the end, is a candidate for cutting.

**Examples.**

Off-voice (padded):
> ChangeDown for Word provides users with the capability to enable AI-based document editing workflows utilizing the native tracked-changes infrastructure built into Microsoft Word.

On-voice:
> ChangeDown lets AI agents edit your Word document. Their changes appear as tracked changes — the ones you already know how to accept or reject.

Off-voice (unnecessarily hedged):
> Your document may potentially be processed using a relay infrastructure that could in certain configurations remain separate from document content.

On-voice:
> The relay never reads a word of your document. It only passes a connection address.

### Confident

Confident means we state things directly. We don't hedge claims we can back up. We don't reach for qualifications when none are warranted. We don't apologize for the product's constraints — we describe them honestly, which is its own kind of confidence.

Confident is not the same as boastful. We are not claiming ChangeDown is the best document editor ever built. We are stating, clearly, what it does and what it doesn't — and trusting the reader to make up her own mind.

Confidence also means we don't panic in the trust sections. The security page doesn't defensively overexplain. It states the facts. The facts are good.

**Examples.**

Off-voice (hedging):
> ChangeDown may be a good option for users who are potentially looking for a way to possibly integrate AI-assisted editing into their existing Word workflows.

On-voice:
> If you work in Word and you want AI edits you can actually review, ChangeDown is for you.

Off-voice (boastful):
> ChangeDown is the most powerful AI document collaboration platform ever built for Microsoft Word.

On-voice:
> Any AI. Real tracked changes. No signup. That's the whole thing.

### Occasionally Dry

Dry doesn't mean cold. It means we don't perform enthusiasm. We let the product speak, and we trust the reader to feel something. A dry sentence can be funny without trying to be funny. It can be warm without saying "welcome." It can be confident without listing adjectives.

Dry moments earn their place. They are not a default register — they show up once or twice on a page, where the product's actual situation is a little absurd or a little neat, and we just name it plainly.

**Examples.**

On-voice (dry, in a good way):
> No signup. No API key. No account to forget the password to.

On-voice:
> Your document stays on your machine. The relay never reads a word of it. This is, we think, how it should work.

On-voice (from the three differentiators):
> Real Word tracked changes — yours forever. Not a proprietary comment thread. Not a list in a dashboard somewhere. Actual tracked changes in your actual document.

## 2. Words and Phrases to Avoid

This section is a hard list. Not a suggestion. If you find yourself reaching for one of these, stop and ask what you actually want to say — then say that.

### Hype words

These words signal that the writer did not trust the product to speak for itself.

| Avoid | Why | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| supercharge | Breathless. Implies the product juices something already working. | Say what it does. |
| unleash | Implies the reader has been holding something back. They haven't. | Say what becomes possible. |
| 10x | A VC deck metric that means nothing to a Word user. | Give a concrete example. |
| transform | Everything transforms everything. | Name the specific change. |
| revolutionize | See "transform." | Same. |
| game-changing | Cliché that actively signals nothing has changed. | Skip it. |
| next-level | Empty. | Skip it. |
| level up | Gaming metaphor that doesn't belong here. | Skip it. |
| cutting-edge | 2012 SaaS. | Skip it. |
| state-of-the-art | 2008 white paper. | Skip it. |
| disruptive | Means the writer is excited. The reader is not. | Skip it. |
| best-in-class | Comparative claim that's never backed up. | Back it up, or remove it. |

**Hype in disguise.** Watch for superlatives that look innocent: "incredibly easy," "surprisingly fast," "beautifully simple." These are hype words wearing adjective clothes. If you need an adverb to sell the adjective, the adjective isn't working.

### SaaS Clichés

These are phrases that might have been fresh once. They are not fresh now. A reader who has clicked on three SaaS landing pages in the past year has seen all of these.

| Avoid | Why |
|---|---|
| first taste | Implies a product is a drug or a dessert sample. Condescending. |
| hit the ground running | Generic. |
| seamlessly | The most overused adverb in software marketing. |
| powerful yet simple | Means nothing. Everything claims this. |
| end-to-end | Means everything and therefore nothing. |
| at the click of a button | Of course you click a button. |
| built for teams | We haven't said who this isn't built for. |
| all in one place | Almost always used to obscure what the thing actually does. |
| out of the box | Lazy shorthand. |
| deep dive | Cliché that sounds more like a TED Talk than a product. |
| pain points | Clinical. Not how people describe their own frustrations. |
| friction | Fine in internal discussion. Not for copy directed at non-developers. |
| workflow | Overused. Use the specific thing: "editing process," "review," "revision." |
| use case | Developer speak. Not for this audience. |
| leverage (as a verb) | Means "use." Use "use." |
| utilize | Same. |

### Wrong Valence

Some words are not wrong in themselves — they carry meaning in other contexts. In this context, they carry the wrong meaning.

**"AI-powered."**
This implies the product is powered by AI — that AI is the engine, the product is the vehicle. That's not what ChangeDown is. ChangeDown is AI-collaborative: AI is a participant. The user is also a participant. The document is the shared space. "AI-powered" flattens the relationship and sounds like every other tool announcing AI features in 2024. We don't say it.

**"Magic."**
Magic means the mechanism is hidden. ChangeDown's whole value proposition is that the mechanism is visible and auditable — tracked changes you can see, accept, or reject, signed by who proposed them. Calling it magic is actively contradictory.

**"Seamless."**
The product does involve steps: opening a room, copying agent instructions, pasting them into your AI. That is not seamless — and calling it seamless would be misleading. The word we want is "straightforward" or just a description of the steps: "Two steps. Copy the instructions. Paste them into your AI."

**"Smart."**
"Smart [noun]" — smart edits, smart review, smart document — is a filler modifier. The edits aren't smart. The AI proposes them. You decide.

**"Collaboration tool."**
Technically accurate. Also what every project management app and video call software calls itself. We have a more specific story to tell.

### Patronizing constructions

These are syntactic patterns more than individual words. They show up when the writer is nervous about whether the reader will understand.

**"It's that simple."** The reader decides what's simple. You don't.

**"Even you can..."** Implies the reader expected to be excluded. Don't.

**"Don't worry..."** Implies there's something to worry about. If there's a concern, address it. If there isn't, don't introduce one.

**"No technical knowledge required."** This is fine on an FAQ when someone asks. It's patronizing in hero copy. Show that it's accessible; don't announce it.

**"You'll love..."** Presumptuous. Let the product make that argument.

## 3. Words and Phrases We Like

These are not slogans or mantras — they're examples of the right kind of language for this product. Use them where they fit. Don't force them.

### Anchoring phrases

**"Pull up a seat."** From the headline. It's invitational without being pushy. It implies the space is set up, ready, waiting — which is accurate (rooms are always open). Use it in welcome contexts. Don't overuse it.

**"Your document."** Not "the document." Not "your file." "Your document" names what's already most important to the reader, and it keeps the user at the center of the story.

**"The way Word has always done them."** Specifically for tracked changes. This is the key reassurance: ChangeDown doesn't invent a new review process. It pipes AI suggestions into the one you already know. This phrase signals that.

**"You decide."** Clean, direct, true. The AI proposes. You decide. The power relationship is explicit. Use it when describing the accept/reject flow.

**"Yours forever."** For tracked changes as durable artifacts. Not stored in our system. Not a comment thread in a proprietary format. Real tracked changes in your real document. "Yours forever" is confident without being dramatic.

**"Any AI."** This is a differentiator. Say it plainly. Not "compatible with leading AI assistants" — just "any AI." Claude. GPT. Whatever you use.

### Structural moves

**Short series of short sentences.** The headline is three beats: *"Pull up a seat. Any AI. Tracked changes in your document."* That structure is available throughout the site. Use it in moderation — it lands hardest when the surrounding text is more expansive.

**The plain claim, followed by what it means.** State the fact, then describe the implication for the reader. "The relay never reads your document. Only a connection address passes through." The second sentence does the work of reassurance without asking you to take the first sentence on faith.

**"Not X. Y."** Useful for contrast. "Not a proprietary comment thread. Real Word tracked changes." The negation makes the affirmative land harder.

## 4. Mascot Voice Rules

### The capybara doesn't speak

The capybara is an editorial illustration. It is present in the design — in the hero, in the footer, in the done-state of the sandbox. It is not a character with dialogue. It does not have a name. It does not have a brand personality separate from ChangeDown's. It does not tweet.

If you find yourself writing a caption, a tooltip, or any copy attributed to the mascot, stop. The mascot's job is to be warm and recognizable without saying anything.

**The voice is the team's, not the mascot's.** This is an important distinction. Mascot-forward voices ("Hi, I'm [name]! I'm here to help you...") put the product behind a character. We are not doing that. The mascot is a guest in the design — it shows up to add warmth, then gets out of the way.

### What the capybara signals

The mascot's presence signals that the product is approachable and a little unusual. Capybaras are known for being calm, unhurried, and comfortable with everyone around them — which happens to be a good metaphor for a tool that lets any AI join your document room without drama. But we do not explain this to the reader. The signal is visual.

The 🦫 emoji appears sparingly in navigation and a few internal documents as an affectionate placeholder. There is no capybara emoji in Unicode. On the live site, the emoji does not appear in consumer-facing copy.

### Poses and context

- **Idle** — used in the hero, FAQ, footer. Default state. The capybara is simply there.
- **Working** — at a small desk with a document. Used on `/install` and `/how-it-works`. Signals "this is where the setup happens."
- **Wave** — turned slightly, hand raised. Used in sandbox scene 7 (done state) and the install CTA section. Not triumphant. More like: "you're all set."

The wave is not a high-five. It's a see-you-later. Keep that reading in mind when writing the surrounding copy.

## 5. Sentence Rhythm

### Short beats over long sentences

The editorial model for this site is closer to a good magazine than a product brochure. Magazine copy breathes. It alternates between short declarative sentences and slightly longer explanatory ones. It doesn't string six clauses together with semicolons.

When in doubt: cut the sentence in half. Put a period in the middle. Read both halves aloud. If they both work, leave them separate.

**Off-voice:**
> ChangeDown for Word is a Microsoft Word task pane that enables users to bring any AI agent — including Claude, ChatGPT, and others — into their live Word documents, where that agent can propose edits that appear as native tracked changes, which the user can then review and accept or reject inline in the document body.

**On-voice:**
> ChangeDown is a Word task pane. You open a room. Your AI joins. Edits arrive as tracked changes in your document — the ones you already know how to accept or reject.

### Pull-quotes earn their italics

Italic formatting is meaningful. We use it in two contexts:

1. **Pull-quotes** — a sentence that deserves to stand alone on the page, set in Source Serif 4 italic. These are used in the trust section and at moments of genuine editorial weight. They are not used to emphasize a word in a sentence ("this is *really* important").

2. **Titles and proper names** — italicize publication names when they appear in running text.

If you're reaching for italics to add emphasis mid-sentence, try rewriting the sentence so emphasis comes from structure, not formatting.

**Off-voice (emphasis overload):**
> ChangeDown is *completely* free for public rooms, and your document data is *never* stored on our servers — it's *always* local to your machine.

**On-voice:**
> Public rooms are free. Your document stays on your machine — the relay only passes a connection address, nothing more.

### No exclamation points except in two cases

1. **Genuine surprise** — something unexpected and good happened. ("The agent joined in 3 seconds!")
2. **Error states** — something went wrong and urgency matters.

In all other cases, no exclamation points. This includes the install success state, the welcome message, the hero headline, and the done-state of the sandbox. The product's confidence does not require punctuation to underscore it.

**Off-voice:**
> You've successfully installed ChangeDown for Word! Welcome to a better way to work!

**On-voice:**
> ChangeDown is installed. Open a free room when you're ready.

### Heading style

Headings are not complete sentences unless they need to be. They are not questions unless the page is an FAQ. They are not imperatives unless they are CTAs.

Good headings for this site are:
- Short noun phrases: "Real tracked changes."
- Incomplete sentences that the body completes: "Any AI. As many as you want."
- Direct statements: "Your document stays on your machine."

Avoid:
- Heading + body as the same complete thought repeated twice
- Questions that the heading then immediately answers
- Headings ending in ellipsis (...) — this is a listicle habit

### CTA language

CTAs are the one place where imperative verbs earn their place. Keep them short and specific. The reader should be able to read the CTA alone and know exactly what happens next.

| Off-voice | On-voice |
|---|---|
| Get started today | Open a free room |
| Learn more | Read the security model |
| Click here to install | Install ChangeDown for Word |
| Try it now for free! | Try it now |
| Discover what's possible | See how it works |

Primary CTAs use copper styling. Ghost CTAs (secondary actions) don't compete. When there are two CTAs side by side, the primary is always the install or room-open action.

## 6. Reading Level and Vocabulary

### Target reader

Senior knowledge worker. Well-educated. Probably has used Word for most of their professional life. May or may not have experimented with AI tools. Skeptical of hype. Has limited patience for copy that doesn't get to the point.

This reader does not know:
- What a "task pane" is (explain it by showing it, not by naming it)
- What "OOXML" means (never mention it)
- What "multi-agent" means as a technical term (use it after explaining what it means: "more than one AI in the same room")
- What "relay" means without context (always pair with: "the connection goes through a relay — a pass-through that never reads your document")

This reader does know:
- Tracked changes — exactly what they are and how to use them
- That accepting and rejecting changes is a familiar Word action
- That AI can write and edit text
- That they have to trust a product before they use it with real documents

### Vocabulary principles

**Use the Word user's vocabulary.** "Accept," "reject," "tracked changes," "revision marks," "the ribbon" — these are native Word terms. Use them. Do not replace them with invented product terms.

**Explain technical terms on first use, then use them freely.** "ChangeDown opens a room — a live channel your AI joins. Once the room is set up, edits arrive instantly." After that, "room" can stand on its own.

**The product's core concepts, defined plainly.**

- **Room** — a live connection space. Your AI joins the room and can propose edits. You're in the room too, through the pane. A "free public room" means it's available immediately, no signup.
- **Pane** — the ChangeDown panel inside Word. You open it from the ribbon. It shows who's in the room, what edits are arriving, what's been reviewed.
- **Tracked change** — you know this one. An edit marked up in the document that you can accept or reject. ChangeDown creates these from AI suggestions, so you review AI edits the way you review anyone else's edits.
- **Agent** — shorthand for an AI working in your document. We use "AI" and "agent" interchangeably in copy for general audiences; "agent" may appear more in technical contexts.

**Avoid jargon that isn't earnable.** "Multi-agent" can appear after you've shown the reader what it means. "Durable provenance" should not appear in consumer copy at all — say "a real tracked change with a real author name, kept in your document."

### Accessibility as a writing practice

Accessibility in writing is not about dumbing down. It's about removing friction for the reader so she spends her energy on the product, not on parsing the copy.

- One idea per sentence.
- Short paragraphs. Three to five sentences is usually the ceiling for body copy.
- Lists when three or more parallel items stack up.
- No walls of text. Every paragraph break is a place to breathe.

## 7. Specific Page Notes

These are short-form reminders for the six v1 pages. Detailed copy will be written separately — these are voice and posture notes.

### Homepage

The homepage's job is to get the reader to understand the product in under 90 seconds and feel confident enough to keep reading (or try the demo). Every section earns that reader's next 15 seconds.

- The hero is three beats. Don't dilute them.
- The sandbox section title ("Try it right now") should be exactly that: an invitation, not a description.
- The three differentiators are not feature descriptions — they're positioning claims. Write them like you believe them.
- The trust line is a pull-quote. It should feel like something a careful person wrote on purpose, not a legal disclaimer.
- The install CTA section is the warmest moment on the page. The copy here can slow down slightly.

### Install page (`/install`)

This is a procedural page. Procedural copy is its own discipline.

- Number the steps. Readers who are doing something want to know where they are.
- Use screenshots. Copy and screenshots work together; the copy should not repeat what the screenshot shows — it should say what the screenshot can't.
- If a step has a gotcha, call it out in a callout. Don't bury it in the step body.
- The page ends with "You're set up." Not "Congratulations!" — the reader did something straightforward, not heroic.

### Security page (`/security`)

The trust page is the most important non-homepage surface. Every reader who visits it is already considering the product — they just want to verify something.

- Lead with the biggest statement first: the relay never reads the document.
- Be specific. "Only a connection address" is better than "minimal data." "Tracked changes are stored in your `.docx` file" is better than "your data is protected."
- Don't defensive-overexplain. Three clear claims, stated plainly, are more credible than six claims with footnotes.
- The page may have a short section on identity and authorship. Write it like a technical explainer, not a legal document.

### FAQ (`/faq`)

Questions and answers. The questions should sound like things actual users say, not marketing questions.

- "How is this different from just using ChatGPT?" — yes.
- "What are the benefits of our innovative multi-agent collaboration infrastructure?" — no.
- Answers should be two to five sentences. If an answer is longer, it probably belongs on a different page (and the FAQ should link there).
- Dry tone works especially well here. The answer to "Does the relay read my document?" should be: "No. It only handles the connection. Your document never leaves your machine."

### Privacy and Terms

These pages are legal documents. They should be written by a lawyer (or reviewed by one). The voice guide applies to the explanatory introductions at the top of each page, and to any plain-English summaries we add. It does not apply to the legal language itself.

Plain-English summary intros for legal pages should follow the same voice as the security page: plain, specific, honest.

## Quick Reference

A single-pass checklist. Read this before filing any copy for review.

### Voice test

- [ ] Does it sound like a person wrote it, not a product announcement?
- [ ] Would a skeptical senior lawyer find it credible?
- [ ] Would a tired editor find it pleasant to read?
- [ ] Does it get to the point before the second sentence?

### Word-level checks

**Remove these unconditionally:**
- supercharge, unleash, revolutionize, transform, game-changing, next-level, cutting-edge, best-in-class
- seamlessly, incredibly, surprisingly, amazingly (and most -ly adverbs modifying adjectives)
- "AI-powered" (use "AI-collaborative" or describe the relationship specifically)
- "magic," "magical"
- "first taste," "pain points," "leverage (v)," "utilize"
- "It's that simple," "You'll love," "Don't worry"

**Reach for these instead:**
- "your document," "you decide," "yours forever"
- "Pull up a seat" (in welcome/hero contexts)
- "any AI"
- "the way Word has always done them"
- short sentences with plain verbs

### Rhythm checks

- [ ] No sentence over ~25 words unless it earns the length
- [ ] Exclamation points only in genuine surprise or error states
- [ ] Italics only for pull-quotes and proper names
- [ ] Headings are noun phrases or short declarative statements, not questions (except FAQ)
- [ ] CTAs are specific about what happens next

### Mascot checks

- [ ] The capybara has no dialogue
- [ ] No caption, tooltip, or copy is attributed to the mascot
- [ ] 🦫 emoji does not appear in consumer-facing copy on the live site

### Reading level checks

- [ ] "Task pane" is explained by showing it, not naming it
- [ ] "Multi-agent" is explained before it's used
- [ ] "Room" is defined on first use
- [ ] No unexplained technical abbreviations (OOXML, CDN, CORS, etc.)

### Trust-page specific

- [ ] Lead with the biggest claim first
- [ ] Claims are specific, not comparative ("only a connection address" not "minimal data")
- [ ] No defensive hedging on things that are genuinely true
